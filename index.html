Explore New Possibilities: The Four Competitive Strokes

Each stroke trains the body differently. Find your best match, then add another when you are ready.

Freestyle (front crawl)

The fastest and most popular stroke. Long, relaxed arm pulls, a steady flutter kick and side breathing every two or three strokes.

Focus: stay long, rotate from the hips

Backstroke

Swim on your back with alternating arms and a flutter kick. Easy on breathing, great for posture and shoulders.

Focus: eyes up, hips high

Breaststroke

Pull, breathe, kick, glide. A slower, rhythmic stroke that many beginners find comfortable.

Focus: timing and a strong glide

Butterfly

The most demanding stroke, with a powerful dolphin kick and both arms moving together. Learn it last.

Focus: rhythm from chest and hips

Make A Choice Today: Your 4-Week Beginner Plan

Three sessions a week, 25 to 35 minutes each. Rest at least one day between sessions and stop if anything hurts. “m” means metres in a standard pool.

Beginner

Short sets, plenty of rest, focus on breathing and floating. Use a kickboard to relax your upper body.

Intermediate

Swim 200 m to 400 m non-stop, add pull-buoy sets and practise bilateral breathing (both sides).

Advanced

Add structured intervals, pace work and stroke variety. Track pace and rest, and plan an easy week every month.

Find Something Better: Five Drills That Fix Your Stroke

Short, focused practice beats long, tired laps. Add one or two drills to every session.

  1. Float and glide

    Push off the wall in a streamlined position and glide as far as you can. This teaches balance and a long body line.

  2. Kick on your side

    Lie on one side with the bottom arm extended and kick for 10 to 15 seconds, then switch. It builds balance and body rotation.

  3. Catch-up

    Hold one hand out front until the other hand touches it. This drill slows your stroke and lengthens your reach.

  4. Fingertip drag

    Drag your fingertips along the water during recovery. It keeps elbows high and the arm relaxed.

  5. Bilateral breathing

    Breathe every three strokes so you alternate sides. It evens out your stroke and helps in open water.

Gear That Actually Helps

You do not need a lot. Start with the basics and add pieces as your training grows.

Goggles

Fit matters more than price. They should seal gently without pressing. Try them on before buying if you can.

Swim cap

Reduces drag and protects long hair from chlorine. Silicone caps last longer and are easier to put on.

Kickboard

Lets you isolate the legs and relax the upper body while you work on your kick.

Pull buoy

Sits between the thighs so you can focus on arms and body position.

Fins

Short training fins build ankle flexibility and kick strength, and help beginners feel the glide.

Water bottle and towel

You sweat in the water too. Keep a bottle at the pool edge and drink between sets.

Six Common Mistakes

Almost every swimmer has made these. Fixing even one makes swimming feel easier.

  • Holding your breath instead of breathing out slowly under water.
  • Lifting your head to breathe and dropping your hips.
  • Kicking from the knees instead of the hips.
  • Crossing your hand over the centre line when you pull.
  • Rushing each stroke instead of reaching long.
  • Skipping warm-up and stretching.

Your Search Ends Here: Swim Safe

Fitness is only fun when you are safe. Keep these rules in mind every time.

  • Swim where a lifeguard is on duty whenever you can.
  • Never swim alone, especially in open water.
  • Check weather, currents and water temperature before entering.
  • Do not swim straight after a heavy meal or after drinking alcohol.
  • Stop and rest if you feel cold, dizzy or very tired.
  • In open water, wear a bright cap and use a tow float.

Frequently asked questions

What is AquaNova?

AquaNova is a swimming and water-fitness guide. It covers strokes, a beginner training plan, drills, gear and safety so you can swim better and with confidence.

Is swimming good exercise?

Yes. Swimming works the whole body, is low impact on joints and helps build endurance and strength. It suits many ages and fitness levels.

How often should a beginner swim?

Two to three sessions a week of 20 to 30 minutes is a good start. Add time or distance slowly and rest between hard sessions.

Why do I get tired so quickly in the pool?

Most new swimmers hold their breath, kick too hard or sit too low in the water. Learning to breathe out steadily and relax makes a big difference.

What gear do I really need?

Goggles that fit well, a swimsuit that suits training and, for long hair, a cap. Kickboards, pull buoys and fins are useful extras.

Is it safe to swim alone?

It is safest to swim with others and where a lifeguard is present. Never swim alone in open water, and check conditions before you enter.
